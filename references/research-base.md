# Research base: ages 4 to 6

What the research supports, and does not support, in each of the thirteen [skill areas](skill-areas.md) for a child of about 4 to 6 years (48-71 months). This is the first band to be answered. The other bands in `skill-areas.md` are still open questions.

Source ids in `code style` point to [research-sources.md](research-sources.md), which gives the citation, link, design, ages studied, finding and limits for each of the 181 sources. Activities built from this page are in [activity-catalog.md](activity-catalog.md).

## How to read this page

Each claim is labelled by type and confidence.

- **Fact** is what a cited source reports.
- **Inference** is this project's reasoning from those sources to a home setting.
- **Speculation** has no reviewed support and is marked so that it can be dropped.
- Confidence is **high**, **moderate** or **low**.

Verification status, as of 2026-11-25: every citation was confirmed to exist by web search. For 83 of the 181 sources, the stored finding was then checked against the paper's own abstract or text (58) or against a secondary summary such as a press release or clearinghouse page (25), and corrected where it was wrong. The other 98 could not be read on that date, mostly because the hosting sites block automated reading; their findings are summarised from prior knowledge, and their limits say so. No paper was read end to end, so open the source before relying on a specific number. [research-sources.md](research-sources.md) labels each source. Volume and page numbers are left out.

This page is educational support for caregivers. It is not an assessment, a screening tool or a list of targets. Monitoring checklists stay separate, as set out in [evidence-policy.md](evidence-policy.md).

## Eight conclusions that cut across areas

1. **A few areas have far better evidence than the rest.** Early number knowledge, code-based reading skills, oral language and fundamental movement skills each have trials or evidence-rated guides behind them. Art, music, cooking and nature have fewer trials, and the ones that exist mostly measure outcomes other than school skills: behaviour, empathy, stress, food acceptance, eyesight. (Fact, high.)
2. **Skill boosts fade unless something keeps building on them.** Gains from early programs usually shrink after the program stops (`bailey-2020`). A modest routine kept up for years is a better bet than an intense push. (Fact for the fade-out, high. Inference for the home routine, moderate.)
3. **Far transfer mostly fails.** Music lessons show no effect, or at most a small one, on intelligence, and the size is disputed (`sala-2020`, `roman-caballero-2022`). Bilingualism does not reliably improve executive function (`lehtonen-2018`, `gunnerud-2020`). Arts programs have not been shown to raise math or reading scores, though a recent trial found gains in writing, behaviour and empathy (`bowen-2023`). Pretend play in general is not shown to drive development (`lillard-2013`). Do these for what they are. (Fact, high for the general pattern; the music question is contested.)
4. **Teach arbitrary codes directly, and guide everything else.** Letter-sound links, numerals, letter formation and movement technique are conventions a child cannot discover, and explicit teaching works for them (`ies-foundational-2016`, `santangelo-2016`, `logan-2012`). For concepts and exploration, guided play, where the adult sets up the goal and the child controls the action, does as well as or better than direct teaching and better than unguided discovery (`skene-2022`, `alfieri-2011`, `weisberg-2016`). (Fact for each part, moderate. The split into two modes is an inference, moderate.)
5. **Do not take over.** Four- and five-year-olds persisted less after an adult stepped in and did a hard task for them (`leonard-2021`). Demonstrating one use of a toy reduced exploration of its other uses (`bonawitz-2011`). Wait, hint, then hand it back. (Fact from single lab studies, moderate.)
6. **Most parent-child correlations overstate what a parent can cause.** Praise style, chores, number talk, puzzle play and spatial talk all predict later outcomes in small correlational studies (`gunderson-2013`, `white-2019`, `levine-2010`, `levine-2012`, `pruden-2011`). Families who do these things differ in many other ways, and children shape what parents do. These are cheap habits worth having. They are not levers. (Inference, high.)
7. **Early predictors are weaker than their reputation.** The marshmallow-test link to later achievement halves in a larger sample and mostly disappears with controls (`watts-2018`). School-entry math predicts later achievement more than anything else measured (`duncan-2007`), yet boosting it does not carry forward at full strength (`bailey-2020`). Stable traits of the child explain most of the link between early and later math (`bailey-2014`). (Fact, high.)
8. **Enrichment correlations are mostly about who enrols.** Much of the link between music lessons and IQ reflects family background, genes and personality (`mosing-2016`, `corrigall-2013`). Large studies showing arts or music students do better at school (`guhn-2020`, `catterall-2012`) cannot separate that. Enrol for the activity, not the correlation. (Fact, moderate.)

## How to divide the week

An earlier version of this page ranked art, music, cooking and a second language as a lower tier, "worth doing for its own sake". That mixed two questions: how strong the evidence is that an area changes other outcomes, and whether the area is worth a child's time. Only the first is a research question. The table states the evidence for each area and the routine shape that suits it, and leaves the choice of what matters to the family. (Inference, moderate.)

| Area | Evidence for practices close to a home routine | Suggested shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reading, math | Trials and rated practice guides | Daily, short |
| Movement | Guidelines and trials of skill teaching | Active every day, skills 2-3 times a week |
| Social-emotional | Trials of parent programs, mostly in selected groups | Woven into the day |
| Music | A learnable skill; short-term social effects; transfer to other skills disputed | Daily singing or rhythm, weekly class or family music |
| Art and drama | School trials with older children; one drama trial at 4-6 | 2-3 sessions a week, a monthly visit or show |
| Writing, building, science | A few trials, mostly correlational at this age | 2-4 times a week |
| Cooking, practical life, nature | Trials for food tasting and outdoor time, otherwise correlational | Daily job and outdoor time, weekly cooking |
| Second language | Needs hours of live exposure to matter | Only if that exposure exists |

## Breadth: meeting many things

This answers a family goal rather than a skill area: a well-rounded child who keeps meeting new things.

**What the evidence supports**

- Interest is built, not found. It starts as situational interest triggered by people and materials and needs outside support before it becomes the child's own (`hidi-renninger-2006`). Theory, moderate.
- Nearly a third of young children develop an intense interest (`deloache-2007`). Early science interests last when families keep supplying chances to learn about them (`alexander-2012`), and curiosity predicts kindergarten reading and math (`shah-2018`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- In sport, world-class adults sampled more sports as children and specialised later than national-level peers (`gullich-2022`), and paediatric guidance warns against early specialisation (`aap-specialization-2016`). Fact, moderate. Retrospective.
- Children in organised activities do better on average, and real over-scheduling is rare (`mahoney-2006`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Practice matters but explains only a minority of differences in performance, about a fifth in music and sport (`macnamara-2014`). Fact, high.

**The strongest case against breadth** (inference, moderate): time is finite, and reading and math have better evidence and compound. Breadth spread too thin can mean no skill gets enough practice to become enjoyable. This library's answer is a short daily core with breadth carried by weekly and monthly rotations, and by following an interest deeply when one appears.

**Activities that carry breadth:** `sel-try-new-activity`, `science-follow-the-interest`, `art-new-medium`, `music-listen-widely`, `art-museum-visit`, `art-live-performance`, `music-live-music`.

## Reading

**What the evidence supports**

- Learning to read an alphabetic script requires explicit teaching of letter-sound links and decoding (`castles-2018`, `ies-foundational-2016`, `nelp-2008`). Fact, high.
- Once a child can decode, growth comes from volume: each word sounded out is a learning trial (`share-1995`, `ehri-2014`), and daily reading of connected text is a rated recommendation (`ies-foundational-2016`). Fact, moderate to high.
- Being read to builds vocabulary and comprehension along a path that is largely separate from decoding practice (`senechal-2002`). Both paths are needed. Fact, moderate.
- Dialogic reading, where the adult asks open questions, helps expressive vocabulary, with a clearly smaller effect at 4-5 than at 2-3 (`mol-2008`). Fact, moderate.
- Back-and-forth conversation, more than the amount of adult talk, relates to language at this age (`romeo-2018`). Fact, low, from one small correlational study.
- Systematic phonics beats unsystematic or no phonics, most when it starts in kindergarten or first grade (`ehri-2001`). Fact, high.
- Decodable text gives immediate gains in accuracy, but early books should meet several criteria, not decodability alone (`cheatham-allor-2012`). Fact, moderate.
- Trained shared-reading programs have a small effect on language, near zero against active comparison activities (`noble-2019`), smaller for older preschoolers (`dowdall-2020`). Fact, moderate. Being read to still matters for volume; technique adds little.
- The amount children read for pleasure tracks comprehension and vocabulary at every age (`mol-bus-2011`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.

**What it does not support**

- Guessing words from pictures or context as a reading strategy (`castles-2018`). High.
- That reading early produces a lasting advantage. In New Zealand, children taught from 7 caught up with children taught from 5 by age 11 (`suggate-2013`). Moderate; non-experimental, and it concerns school start age, not a child who is already reading and enjoys it.

**For a child who already decodes before kindergarten** (inference, moderate): the school-age guidance applies by skill, not age. Keep decoding practice matched to the patterns the child knows, add reading volume, keep read-alouds far above their decoding level, and pair reading with spelling by sound.

**Resources:** a decodable book series matched to the child's known patterns; early-reader series for the step after decodables; library read-alouds, including nonfiction; magnetic letters or letter tiles; a scripted phonics program if the adult wants a sequence to follow.

**Activities:** `reading-decodable-daily`, `reading-aloud-above-level`, `reading-word-building`, `reading-sound-games`, `reading-retell`, `reading-conversation-turns`.

## Writing

**What the evidence supports**

- Name writing and early writing are among the preschool predictors of later literacy (`nelp-2008`), and writing knowledge develops in a consistent order with name writing in the lead (`puranik-2011`). Fact, moderate.
- How well a kindergartner spells by sound predicts first-grade reading and spelling beyond sound awareness and letter knowledge (`ouellette-2017`). Fact, moderate.
- From kindergarten on, explicit handwriting teaching improves legibility, fluency and the quality of what children write (`santangelo-2016`, `ies-writers-2012`). Fact, moderate.
- Fine motor skill at school entry predicts later achievement (`grissmer-2010`). Fact, moderate. It is correlational.
- Spelling by sound, with feedback, is causal for early reading. Kindergartners with weak sound awareness who practised it learned to read more words than those given sound training or storybooks (`senechal-2012`, `ouellette-2008`). Fact, moderate. Small trials.
- Writing instruction in preschool improves letter knowledge and sound awareness (`hall-2015`), and from first grade writing instruction improves reading (`graham-hebert-2011`). Fact, moderate.
- Printing letters by hand, not typing or tracing them, changed how 4- and 5-year-olds' brains responded to letters (`james-2012`). Fact, low. Tiny imaging study.

**What it does not support**

- That training fine motor skill raises achievement. No reviewed study shows it.
- Formal handwriting programs before kindergarten. The reviewed studies start at kindergarten, so starting earlier is an extrapolation.

**For this band** (inference, moderate): separate composing from handwriting. A child of 4 or 5 can spell with letter tiles and dictate stories well beyond what their hand can write.

**Resources:** wide-ruled paper, short pencils and crayons, a small whiteboard, sticky notes for labels, child scissors, clay, tongs, a structured handwriting sequence if wanted.

**Activities:** `writing-invented-spelling`, `writing-letter-formation`, `writing-dictated-story`, `writing-fine-motor-work`, `writing-name-and-labels`.

## Math

**What the evidence supports**

- Math skill at school entry is the strongest measured predictor of later achievement (`duncan-2007`). Fact, high. It is a correlation.
- Teaching number and operations along a developmental progression is the one early-math recommendation with more than minimal evidence (`ies-math-2013`). Fact, high.
- About an hour in total of a linear numbered board game improved four number skills in low-income preschoolers, and the gain lasted nine weeks (`siegler-2008`, `ramani-2008`). Fact, high. This is the best-tested home-style activity in this library.
- Short shared story problems over a school year raised first-graders' math achievement (`berkowitz-2015`). Fact, moderate.
- Early math is wider than counting and includes shape, space, pattern and measurement (`clements-2011`, `ies-math-2013`). Fact, moderate. Evidence for teaching those strands is rated minimal.
- Kindergarten number skill predicts math through third grade (`jordan-2009`), and math at 54 months, and especially growth to first grade, predicts math at 15 (`watts-2014`). Fact, high. Correlational.
- Preschool counting, quantity and repeating-pattern knowledge predicted fifth-grade math in low-income children (`rittle-johnson-2017`). Fact, moderate.
- Home math activities have a small positive association with math achievement (`daucourt-2021`). A math-anxious parent who helps often with homework was linked to children learning less (`maloney-2015`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.

**What it does not support**

- That an early lead in math is kept without continued input (`bailey-2020`). Stable child traits explain much of the early-later link (`bailey-2014`). High.
- That the board-game effect holds for children who already know numbers to 10 well. They may be at ceiling. Inference, moderate.

**Resources:** a home-made numbered board (1-10, then 1-100 in rows of ten), dice, dominoes, dot cards, ten-frames, a kitchen scale, measuring cups, a daily story-problem habit or app, family math material from university early-math projects.

**Activities:** `math-linear-board-game`, `math-count-and-cardinality`, `math-subitize-dots`, `math-story-problems`, `math-measure-compare`, `math-patterns`.

## Science

**What the evidence supports**

- Preschoolers already reason from evidence: they test ideas, learn from their own interventions and infer causes (`gopnik-2012`). Fact, moderate.
- They ask a very large number of information-seeking questions and persist until answered (`chouinard-2007`). Fact, moderate.
- Showing a child exactly what a new object does reduces how much else they discover about it (`bonawitz-2011`). Fact, moderate, from one lab study.
- Guided discovery beats unassisted discovery (`alfieri-2011`), and guided play matches or beats direct instruction on some outcomes (`skene-2022`). Fact, moderate.
- For a procedural skill such as running a fair test, direct instruction taught many more 8- to 10-year-olds than discovery, and those who learned it either way used it equally well (`klahr-nigam-2004`). Fact, high for that skill and age.
- Preschoolers explore more when evidence is ambiguous (`schulz-bonawitz-2007`), and explaining a surprising result steers what they test next (`legare-2014`). They notice and push back when a 'why' question gets a non-answer (`frazier-2009`). Fact, moderate.
- Science gaps at third grade are largely explained by general knowledge, reading and math at kindergarten entry (`morgan-2016`). Fact, moderate. Correlational. General knowledge of the world is the preschool route into science.
- Parents explained science three times as often to boys as to girls at museum exhibits (`crowley-2001`). Fact, moderate. A bias worth checking in oneself.
- Curiosity can be defined and measured at this age as the uncertainty a child will tolerate before exploring (`jirout-klahr-2012`). Theory, moderate.

**What it does not support**

- Leaving children to work science out alone. Unassisted discovery does worse than explicit teaching (`alfieri-2011`). High.
- Any specific home science curriculum at this age. None was reviewed.

**Resources:** a magnifier, magnets, a ramp and balls, a tub of water, ice, a kitchen scale, a notebook, library nonfiction.

**Activities:** `science-predict-test`, `science-explore-first`, `science-why-chain`, `science-observe-record`, `science-follow-the-interest`.

## Art and drama

The strongest point against: evidence that art at home changes anything beyond art is thin, and almost all of it comes from school programs with older or disadvantaged children. The earlier version of this page went further and said there was close to nothing. That was wrong. It left out the randomised trials below.

**What the evidence supports**

- School arts programs have causal effects in recent randomised trials. In 42 Houston schools, more arts learning reduced disciplinary infractions and raised writing achievement and emotional empathy (`bowen-2023`). A lottery-assigned art museum visit improved critical thinking about art, most for younger and disadvantaged students (`bowen-2014`). Live theatre raised tolerance, perspective taking and plot knowledge where the film version did not (`greene-2017`). Fact, moderate to high for those settings. School-age children; read from abstracts and summaries.
- At this age, eight weeks of dramatic pretend play games improved emotional self-control, with no effect on empathy or social skills (`goldstein-2018`). Fact, moderate. The closest arts trial to a 4- to 6-year-old.
- Daily arts classes in a Head Start preschool went with larger vocabularies and better emotion regulation (`brown-2010`, `brown-sax-2013`) and with lower cortisol after arts classes than after ordinary classes (`brown-2017`). Fact, low to moderate. The first two are not randomised.
- Drawing is a working tool for perception, memory and communication (`fan-2023`). Five- and six-year-olds recalled more of an outing when they drew it while telling it (`butler-1995`). Fact, low to moderate.
- Creativity training works when it teaches finding problems and combining ideas (`scott-2004`). Fact, moderate. Mostly older learners.
- Arts-involved low-income students do better at school (`catterall-2012`), and eminent scientists are more likely than other scientists to have arts hobbies (`root-bernstein-2008`). Fact, low weight. Selection explains an unknown share.

**What it does not support**

- That art raises math or reading scores. The large review that predates the trials found little rigorous evidence for it (`winner-2013`), and the Houston trial reports gains in writing, behaviour and empathy, not math or reading. Moderate.
- That drama reliably builds language. Studies link the two but are too flawed to rely on (`podlozny-2000`, `mages-2008`). Moderate.
- That a creative dance program builds social competence. A very small trial found no significant effect (`lobo-2006`). Low.

**What a parent can act on** (inference, moderate): keep real materials in reach and describe the work rather than gush, since inflated praise led children with low self-esteem to avoid harder tasks (`brummelman-2014`). Add structured drama games, the one arts practice tested at this age. Take the child to real art and live performance, since the strongest trials are of exposure, not of making. Expect drawing to fade with age unless adults keep it going (`rose-2006`).

**Resources:** paint, clay, card, tape, glue, good child scissors and decent paper in reach; a sketchbook; an art museum (many are free); children's theatre and library programs.

**Activities:** `art-open-materials`, `art-new-medium`, `art-observational-drawing`, `art-draw-to-remember`, `art-cut-fold-construct`, `art-look-and-talk`, `art-museum-visit`, `art-drama-games`, `art-act-out-stories`, `art-live-performance`.

## Music

The strongest point against: if the aim is a smarter child or better school results, music lessons are a weak bet. That is not this library's reason for music, and the evidence for music's own value is better than the earlier version of this page said.

**What the evidence supports**

- Music is a skill that is built by use and lost without it. Singing in tune improves from kindergarten through late elementary school, and adults who stopped singing perform at about the kindergarten level (`demorest-2015`). Children's brains adapt to 15 months of instrument training in step with their musical gains (`hyde-2009`). Over a year, children in an intensive program gained in pitch skills while comparison children declined in singing (`ilari-2016`). Fact, moderate.
- How a beginner practises predicts progress more than how long they practise (`mcpherson-2005`), and practice explains about a fifth of differences in music performance (`macnamara-2014`). Fact, moderate. The first is from prior knowledge.
- Making music together has short-term social effects. Four-year-olds helped and cooperated more straight after joint music making than after a matched game (`kirschner-2010`), and a school year of group music games raised older children's empathy (`rabinowitch-2013`). Fact, low to moderate. Lasting effects are unproven.
- Preschoolers had lower cortisol after music, dance and art classes than after ordinary classes (`brown-2017`). Fact, moderate. A stress marker, in a disadvantaged sample.
- Music and language share some machinery. A preschool music program improved awareness of words and syllables as much as a phonological program (`dege-2011`). Beat-keeping goes with sharper neural encoding of speech (`woodruff-2014`). Training has small effects on auditory and language measures (`neves-2022`) and on sound awareness, but not on reading (`gordon-2015`). Music playschool children gained more in sound awareness and vocabulary than dance or no-class groups (`linnavalli-2018`). Fact, low to moderate. Small effects; publication bias possible.
- Shared music at home at 2-3 has small associations with later vocabulary, numeracy, attention regulation and prosocial skill (`williams-2015`). Fact, low. Correlational.

**What is disputed**

- Transfer to IQ and school results. A meta-analysis limited to well-controlled trials finds no effect (`sala-2020`); reanalyses with different choices find a small positive one (`roman-caballero-2022`, `bigand-tillmann-2022`). Single trials point both ways: a small IQ gain after a year of lessons (`schellenberg-2004`) and short-term verbal gains (`moreno-2011`), against no benefit from preschool music classes in two trials (`mehr-2013`), no lasting advantage from three years of piano (`costa-giomi-1999`) and contested neural results from a community program (`kraus-2014`). Twin and personality studies suggest much of the music-IQ correlation reflects who takes lessons (`mosing-2016`, `corrigall-2013`), which also undercuts large correlational studies (`guhn-2020`). Verdict (inference, moderate): any general cognitive effect is small and may be zero. It is not a reason to start or to stop.

**What it does not support**

- Starting lessons early for the sake of intelligence or school results.
- Brief classes or apps producing cognitive gains (`mehr-2013`).

**For a family that values music for breadth** (inference, moderate): the defensible aims are musical skill (singing in tune, keeping a beat, playing an instrument), the habit of making music with others, and wide listening so that an interest has a chance to form. All three are reachable at 4-5 with a few minutes a day, a weekly group class, and regular live and recorded music. Individual lessons are worth starting when the child asks for them and returns to an instrument unprompted.

**Resources:** daily singing; a keyboard, ukulele or xylophone in reach; a weekly group class; a varied playlist; free family concerts.

**Activities:** `music-steady-beat`, `music-sing-daily`, `music-pitch-match`, `music-make-together`, `music-group-class`, `music-listen-widely`, `music-move-to-music`, `music-live-music`, `music-instrument-exposure`, `music-lessons-decision`.

## Movement and sports

**What the evidence supports**

- Children aged 3-4 should get at least 180 minutes of activity a day with 60 of them moderate-to-vigorous, and from 6 at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity (`who-2019`, `us-pag-2018`). Fact, high for the recommendation, moderate for the evidence under it.
- Fundamental movement skills (throw, catch, kick, strike, run, jump, hop, balance) improve with instruction and practice and did not improve with free play alone (`logan-2012`). Fact, moderate.
- Children with better movement skills are more active and fitter later, and the link strengthens with age (`stodden-2008`, `robinson-2015`). Fact, moderate. Mostly correlational.
- Organized sport is beneficial when it stays fun and fits the child's development, and sampling several sports beats early specialization (`aap-sports-2019`, `aap-specialization-2016`). Fact, moderate.
- Swim lessons at ages 1-4 were associated with much lower drowning risk, and paediatric guidance recommends them as one layer of protection (`brenner-2009`, `aap-drowning-2019`). Fact, moderate. The estimate is imprecise.
- Risky outdoor play is associated with more activity and better social health without more fractures (`brussoni-2015`). Fact, moderate. Observational. A theoretical argument holds that it also lets children master fears (`sandseter-2011`); that part is untested.
- Structured movement-skill programs in childcare and kindergarten improve movement skills (`wick-2017`). Activity in the early years goes with better motor development and fitness (`carson-2017`). Fact, moderate.
- Sport participation goes with higher self-esteem, better social interaction and fewer depressive symptoms, more for team sport (`eime-2013`). Fact, moderate. Mostly cross-sectional.
- Children say sport is fun when they try hard, are coached positively and get better (`visek-2015`). Fact, low. Older children.
- World-class athletes sampled more sports as children (`gullich-2022`), and specialised young athletes who trained more hours a week than their age had more serious overuse injuries (`jayanthi-2015`). Fact, moderate.

**What it does not support**

- Early specialization in one sport (`aap-specialization-2016`). High.
- Expecting team tactics from most children under about 6 (`aap-sports-2019`). Moderate. The age figure is recalled from memory.

**For a child who wants team sports before 6** (inference, moderate): say yes to low-key leagues, coach skills more than strategy, and teach the object-control skills at home, since those are what make games fun and are what free play does not supply.

**Resources:** balls of several sizes, a tee and bat, a low hoop, cones, a bike, swim lessons, a recreation league that changes sport by season.

**Activities:** `movement-throw-catch`, `movement-kick-strike-dribble`, `movement-locomotor-balance`, `movement-active-hour`, `movement-swim`, `movement-team-play`, `movement-risky-play`.

## Social-emotional skills

**What the evidence supports**

- Social competence and self-control in early childhood predict adult outcomes decades later (`jones-2015`, `moffitt-2011`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Emotion knowledge and regulation at 3-4 predict social competence in kindergarten (`denham-2003`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Adults taking over reduces persistence (`leonard-2021`). Fact, moderate.
- Parents' support for autonomy is related to better achievement and wellbeing (`vasquez-2016`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Some activities improve executive function when the challenge keeps rising, with narrow transfer (`diamond-2011`). Fact, moderate. Later results for several programs are mixed.
- Parents who keep directing an already engaged kindergartner have children with poorer self-regulation (`obradovic-2021`), and overcontrolling parenting at 2 predicted poorer regulation at 5 and more problems at 10 (`perry-2018`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Infants tried harder after watching an adult struggle and succeed than after watching easy success (`leonard-2017`). Fact, moderate. Infants.
- Parent programs work: teaching parents of very shy preschoolers to reduce overprotection and encourage gradual steps cut later anxiety diagnoses (`rapee-2005`); a parent-only program that reduced accommodation matched therapy for anxious older children (`lebowitz-2020`); a six-session emotion-coaching program reduced preschool behaviour problems (`havighurst-2010`). Fact, high for selected groups.
- School and preschool social-emotional programs improve skills and behaviour (`durlak-2011`, `murano-2020`). Fact, moderate to high. Classroom programs.
- Praise for intelligence lowered persistence after failure in older children (`mueller-dweck-1998`), but replications failed (`li-bates-2019`). Inflated praise led children with low self-esteem to avoid challenge (`brummelman-2014`). Fact, moderate. The safe rule is specific and accurate praise.

**What it does not support**

- Treating delay of gratification as a lever (`watts-2018`). High.
- Large effects from growth-mindset praise. Mindset effects on achievement are very small (`sisk-2018`). High.
- That social-emotional behaviour at school entry predicts later academic achievement. It did not in six datasets (`duncan-2007`). It predicts other things (`jones-2015`).

**On pushing a child outside their comfort zone** (inference, moderate): the best evidence is from parent programs for shy or anxious children, where the active ingredients are less overprotection, less accommodation, gradual steps toward what is feared, and visible confidence in the child (`rapee-2005`, `lebowitz-2020`). No study tests the same routine in a typical child, so `sel-brave-thing` is graded `adapted`. A broader argument that children have lost independent activity and that this harms wellbeing (`gray-2023`) is plausible but rests on trends and correlations.

**Resources:** simple board and card games with turns and a winner, picture books with strong feelings in them, a habit of waiting before helping.

**Activities:** `sel-let-them-struggle`, `sel-process-praise`, `sel-feeling-words`, `sel-rule-games`, `sel-brave-thing`, `sel-plan-do-review`, `sel-try-new-activity`.

## Practical life and independence

**What the evidence supports**

- Chores in kindergarten are associated with later self-competence, prosocial behaviour and math scores (`white-2019`), and chores relate to executive function in a cross-sectional survey (`tepper-2022`). Fact, low. Correlational, and not all studies agree.
- A Montessori preschool program, of which practical-life work is one part, did well in a lottery study (`lillard-2017`, `lillard-2006`), and Montessori education outperforms traditional schooling on academic and nonacademic outcomes across 32 studies (`randolph-2023`). Fact, moderate. None of this can be attributed to practical-life work.
- How you ask matters. 'Be a helper' raised helping in 3- to 6-year-olds (`bryan-2014`) but made 4- and 5-year-olds give up more after a setback (`foster-hanson-2020`). Material rewards reduced toddlers' later helping (`warneken-2008`). Fact, moderate. Lab tasks.
- In some communities children take initiative in complex household work rather than being assigned chores (`coppens-2014`). Fact, low. Small and descriptive.

**What it does not support**

- That chores cause later success. The widely repeated claim rests on correlations. High.

**Why include it anyway** (inference, moderate): the skills are useful in themselves, the cost is low, and doing a real job without an adult taking over is the same mechanism as in `leonard-2021`.

**Resources:** tools the child can reach and manage: a small jug, cloths, a child-size broom, a step stool, low hooks, a picture checklist by the door.

**Activities:** `practical-daily-job`, `practical-self-care-chain`, `practical-pour-carry-clean`, `practical-errand`.

## Cooking

**What the evidence supports**

- Repeated tasting works. A small taste of one vegetable every day for 14 days increased 2- to 6-year-olds' liking and intake of it; information alone did not (`wardle-2003`). Fact, high.
- Pressure backfires. Preschoolers ate more, and complained less, when not pressured to eat (`galloway-2006`), and controlling feeding strategies look counterproductive across 120 experiments (`decosta-2017`). Fact, moderate.
- Cooking increases willingness. Children who cooked chose and tasted more unfamiliar vegetable foods (`allirot-2016`) and ate more of the meal they made (`vanderhorst-2014`), and cooking programs improve food preferences (`hersch-2014`). Fact, moderate. Older children, short studies.
- Three or more family meals a week go with lower odds of overweight and unhealthy eating (`hammons-fiese-2011`), and children who help prepare meals prefer vegetables more (`chu-2013`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.

**What it does not support**

- Any link from cooking to academic skills. Counting and measuring while cooking is math practice in a kitchen (inference, moderate).

**Not yet reviewed:** age guidance for knives and heat.

**Resources:** a child-safe knife, a peeler, a stable step, measuring cups and spoons, a few picture recipes.

**Activities:** `cooking-prep-vegetables`, `cooking-measure-recipe`, `cooking-mix-crack-spread`, `cooking-taste-new-foods`.

## Nature and outdoors

**What the evidence supports**

- An extra 40 minutes outdoors each school day reduced new cases of short-sightedness over three years in a randomized trial of six-year-olds in China (`he-2015`). Fact, high for that population. Transfer to other populations is an inference, moderate.
- A second school trial found the same in Taiwan, in children already short-sighted and not (`wu-2018`), and a meta-analysis puts the reduction in new cases at roughly 45%, without slowing progression in eyes already short-sighted (`xiong-2017`). Fact, high.
- More outdoor time at Norwegian daycare went with better working memory and fewer attention problems from 4 to 7 (`ulset-2017`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- About half of reported links between nature contact and children's mental health are positive and half are null (`tillmann-2018`). Fact, moderate. Mostly cross-sectional.
- Children with ADHD concentrated better after a walk in a park than in town (`faber-taylor-2009`). Fact, low. Seventeen children.
- Unstructured nature play is associated with more physical activity and better social and emotional outcomes (`dankiw-2020`). Fact, low.
- A narrative review argues that nature experience improves attention and learning (`kuo-2019`). Fact that the argument is made, low weight.

**What it does not support**

- Strong claims that nature time improves learning. The evidence is weak.

**Resources:** clothes for all weather, a magnifier, a notebook, a bucket, a local place to return to, library nonfiction and field guides.

**Activities:** `nature-outdoor-time`, `nature-notice-walk`, `nature-grow-and-care`, `nature-free-play`, `nature-animal-study`.

## Building and engineering

**What the evidence supports**

- Spatial skills improve with training by nearly half a standard deviation on average, and the gains last and transfer to other spatial tasks (`uttal-2013`). Fact, high.
- Spatial training improves math performance by about a quarter of a standard deviation, more so with physical materials (`hawes-2022`). Fact, moderate. Preschool evidence is thin.
- Copying block constructions relates to early math in three-year-olds (`verdine-2014`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Puzzle play and parents' spatial words predict later spatial skill (`levine-2012`, `pruden-2011`). Fact, low. Correlational.
- Guided play improves spatial vocabulary more than free play (`skene-2022`), and 4- and 5-year-olds understood shapes better after guided play than after direct teaching or free play (`fisher-2013`). Fact, moderate.
- A kindergarten block-building program, rated as a well-run randomised trial, improved spatial skills (`casey-2008`). Fact, moderate.
- Children who play with puzzles, blocks or board games most often score higher on spatial tests (`jirout-newcombe-2015`), and block-building complexity at 4 predicted math at grade 7 and in high school, though not at grades 3 or 5 (`wolfgang-2001`). Fact, low. Correlational.
- Spatial ability in adolescence predicts STEM degrees and careers beyond math and verbal ability (`wai-2009`). Fact, high. It says nothing about training it at 4.

**What it does not support**

- That owning building toys is enough. The measured skills are copying a model, rotating shapes and using spatial words, which free building may or may not exercise. Inference, moderate.

**Resources:** unit blocks, interlocking bricks, magnetic tiles, jigsaws of rising piece count, tangrams, pattern blocks, cardboard and tape.

**Activities:** `building-copy-model`, `building-puzzles`, `building-spatial-talk`, `building-design-challenge`, `building-maps`.

## Second language (optional)

**What the evidence supports**

- Children are not confused by two languages, and how well each is learned depends on the amount and quality of exposure (`byers-2013`). Fact, moderate.
- Infants learned a new language's sounds from a live person and not from recordings (`kuhl-2003`). Fact, moderate, for infants.
- Native-like grammar required starting by about 10-12 in a very large online sample (`hartshorne-2018`). Fact, moderate.
- Each language grows with its share of exposure; toddlers learning two languages are behind in each one but not in total vocabulary (`hoff-2012`). Fact, moderate.
- Intensive daily play sessions with live tutors taught Spanish toddlers English words, and the gain held (`ferjan-ramirez-2017`). Fact, moderate. An hour a day, not a weekly class.
- German pupils who started school English in grade 1 were overtaken by grade-3 starters by grade 7 (`jaekel-2017`). Fact, moderate. Early starts with little exposure do not pay.

**What it does not support**

- A general cognitive advantage from bilingualism (`lehtonen-2018` for adults, `gunnerud-2020` for children). Positive results were more likely to be published (`de-bruin-2015`). High for adults, moderate for children.
- Proficiency from songs, apps or a weekly class alone. Inference, high, from the exposure findings.

**Decision rule** (inference, moderate): take this on only if the family can supply hours a week of live interaction with a fluent speaker for years. Otherwise the same time is better spent elsewhere.

**Activities:** `language2-live-speaker`, `language2-songs-routines`, `language2-books-with-adult`.

## What is still unknown

- Whether any of these home routines, as written, changes long-run outcomes. None has been tested in the form given here.
- The right dose. Minutes and frequency in the catalog are practical suggestions, not findings.
- Sleep, screen use and nutrition were not reviewed and are outside the thirteen areas.
- Bands other than 48-71 months.
- Whether the 98 summaries not yet checked against their sources are accurate. They are labelled in `research-sources.md` and are the next sources to read.
- Sources flagged in `research-sources.md` as recalled from memory or contested by work that was not reviewed.
