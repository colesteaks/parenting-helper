# Research base: ages 4 to 6

What the research supports, and does not support, in each of the thirteen [skill areas](skill-areas.md) for a child of about 4 to 6 years (48-71 months). This is the first band to be answered. The other bands in `skill-areas.md` are still open questions.

Source ids in `code style` point to [research-sources.md](research-sources.md), which gives the citation, link, design, ages studied, finding and limits for each of the 71 sources. Activities built from this page are in [activity-catalog.md](activity-catalog.md).

## How to read this page

Each claim is labelled by type and confidence.

- **Fact** is what a cited source reports.
- **Inference** is this project's reasoning from those sources to a home setting.
- **Speculation** has no reviewed support and is marked so that it can be dropped.
- Confidence is **high**, **moderate** or **low**.

Verification status, as of 2026-11-25: every citation was confirmed to exist by web search. Findings are summarised from prior knowledge of each source. No full text was re-read on that date, so open the source before relying on a specific number. Volume and page numbers are left out because they were not confirmed.

This page is educational support for caregivers. It is not an assessment, a screening tool or a list of targets. Monitoring checklists stay separate, as set out in [evidence-policy.md](evidence-policy.md).

## Seven conclusions that cut across areas

1. **A few areas have far better evidence than the rest.** Early number knowledge, code-based reading skills, oral language and fundamental movement skills each have trials or evidence-rated guides behind them. Art, music, cooking and nature have little or none for wider benefits. (Fact, high.)
2. **Skill boosts fade unless something keeps building on them.** Gains from early programs usually shrink after the program stops (`bailey-2020`). A modest routine kept up for years is a better bet than an intense push. (Fact for the fade-out, high. Inference for the home routine, moderate.)
3. **Far transfer mostly fails.** Music lessons do not raise intelligence in controlled studies (`sala-2020`). Bilingualism does not reliably improve executive function (`lehtonen-2018`, `gunnerud-2020`). Arts education does not raise other school results (`winner-2013`). Pretend play is not shown to drive development (`lillard-2013`). Do these for what they are. (Fact, high.)
4. **Teach arbitrary codes directly, and guide everything else.** Letter-sound links, numerals, letter formation and movement technique are conventions a child cannot discover, and explicit teaching works for them (`ies-foundational-2016`, `santangelo-2016`, `logan-2012`). For concepts and exploration, guided play, where the adult sets up the goal and the child controls the action, does as well as or better than direct teaching and better than unguided discovery (`skene-2022`, `alfieri-2011`, `weisberg-2016`). (Fact for each part, moderate. The split into two modes is an inference, moderate.)
5. **Do not take over.** Four- and five-year-olds persisted less after an adult stepped in and did a hard task for them (`leonard-2021`). Demonstrating one use of a toy reduced exploration of its other uses (`bonawitz-2011`). Wait, hint, then hand it back. (Fact from single lab studies, moderate.)
6. **Most parent-child correlations overstate what a parent can cause.** Praise style, chores, number talk, puzzle play and spatial talk all predict later outcomes in small correlational studies (`gunderson-2013`, `white-2019`, `levine-2010`, `levine-2012`, `pruden-2011`). Families who do these things differ in many other ways, and children shape what parents do. These are cheap habits worth having. They are not levers. (Inference, high.)
7. **Early predictors are weaker than their reputation.** The marshmallow-test link to later achievement halves in a larger sample and mostly disappears with controls (`watts-2018`). School-entry math predicts later achievement more than anything else measured (`duncan-2007`), yet boosting it does not carry forward at full strength (`bailey-2020`). (Fact, high.)

## Where to put effort first

This ranking is an inference (moderate confidence) from the strength of evidence and how directly a caregiver can act on it. It is not a statement about what matters most in a life.

| Tier | Areas | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Best evidence, daily habit | Reading, math, movement, social-emotional | Trials or rated guides exist for practices close to home routines. |
| 2. Good reason, weaker proof | Building, writing, science, practical life, nature | Trainable skills or strong correlates, with home routines untested. |
| 3. Worth doing for its own sake | Art, music, cooking, second language | Little or no evidence of wider benefit. The value is the skill and the enjoyment. |

## Reading

**What the evidence supports**

- Learning to read an alphabetic script requires explicit teaching of letter-sound links and decoding (`castles-2018`, `ies-foundational-2016`, `nelp-2008`). Fact, high.
- Once a child can decode, growth comes from volume: each word sounded out is a learning trial (`share-1995`, `ehri-2014`), and daily reading of connected text is a rated recommendation (`ies-foundational-2016`). Fact, moderate to high.
- Being read to builds vocabulary and comprehension along a path that is largely separate from decoding practice (`senechal-2002`). Both paths are needed. Fact, moderate.
- Dialogic reading, where the adult asks open questions, helps expressive vocabulary, with a clearly smaller effect at 4-5 than at 2-3 (`mol-2008`). Fact, moderate.
- Back-and-forth conversation, more than the amount of adult talk, relates to language at this age (`romeo-2018`). Fact, low, from one small correlational study.

**What it does not support**

- Guessing words from pictures or context as a reading strategy (`castles-2018`). High.
- Any claim that reading early produces a lasting advantage. This project reviewed no evidence on that question. Unknown.

**For a child who already decodes before kindergarten** (inference, moderate): the school-age guidance applies by skill, not age. Keep decoding practice matched to the patterns the child knows, add reading volume, keep read-alouds far above their decoding level, and pair reading with spelling by sound.

**Resources:** a decodable book series matched to the child's known patterns; early-reader series for the step after decodables; library read-alouds, including nonfiction; magnetic letters or letter tiles; a scripted phonics program if the adult wants a sequence to follow.

**Activities:** `reading-decodable-daily`, `reading-aloud-above-level`, `reading-word-building`, `reading-sound-games`, `reading-retell`, `reading-conversation-turns`.

## Writing

**What the evidence supports**

- Name writing and early writing are among the preschool predictors of later literacy (`nelp-2008`), and writing knowledge develops in a consistent order with name writing in the lead (`puranik-2011`). Fact, moderate.
- How well a kindergartner spells by sound predicts first-grade reading and spelling beyond sound awareness and letter knowledge (`ouellette-2017`). Fact, moderate.
- From kindergarten on, explicit handwriting teaching improves legibility, fluency and the quality of what children write (`santangelo-2016`, `ies-writers-2012`). Fact, moderate.
- Fine motor skill at school entry predicts later achievement (`grissmer-2010`). Fact, moderate. It is correlational.

**What it does not support**

- That training fine motor skill raises achievement. No reviewed study shows it.
- Formal handwriting programs before kindergarten. The reviewed studies start at kindergarten, so starting earlier is an extrapolation.

**For this band** (inference, moderate): separate composing from handwriting. A child of 4 or 5 can spell with letter tiles and dictate stories well beyond what their hand can write.

**Resources:** wide-ruled paper, short pencils and crayons, a small whiteboard, sticky notes for labels, child scissors, clay, tongs, a structured handwriting sequence if wanted.

**Activities:** `writing-invented-spelling`, `writing-letter-formation`, `writing-dictated-story`, `writing-fine-motor-work`, `writing-name-and-labels`.

## Math

**What the evidence supports**

- Math skill at school entry is the strongest measured predictor of later achievement (`duncan-2007`). Fact, high. It is a correlation.
- Teaching number and operations along a developmental progression is the one early-math recommendation with more than minimal evidence (`ies-math-2013`). Fact, high.
- About an hour in total of a linear numbered board game improved four number skills in low-income preschoolers, and the gain lasted nine weeks (`siegler-2008`, `ramani-2008`). Fact, high. This is the best-tested home-style activity in this library.
- Short shared story problems over a school year raised first-graders' math achievement (`berkowitz-2015`). Fact, moderate.
- Early math is wider than counting and includes shape, space, pattern and measurement (`clements-2011`, `ies-math-2013`). Fact, moderate. Evidence for teaching those strands is rated minimal.

**What it does not support**

- That an early lead in math is kept without continued input (`bailey-2020`). High.
- That the board-game effect holds for children who already know numbers to 10 well. They may be at ceiling. Inference, moderate.

**Resources:** a home-made numbered board (1-10, then 1-100 in rows of ten), dice, dominoes, dot cards, ten-frames, a kitchen scale, measuring cups, a daily story-problem habit or app, family math material from university early-math projects.

**Activities:** `math-linear-board-game`, `math-count-and-cardinality`, `math-subitize-dots`, `math-story-problems`, `math-measure-compare`, `math-patterns`.

## Science

**What the evidence supports**

- Preschoolers already reason from evidence: they test ideas, learn from their own interventions and infer causes (`gopnik-2012`). Fact, moderate.
- They ask a very large number of information-seeking questions and persist until answered (`chouinard-2007`). Fact, moderate.
- Showing a child exactly what a new object does reduces how much else they discover about it (`bonawitz-2011`). Fact, moderate, from one lab study.
- Guided discovery beats unassisted discovery (`alfieri-2011`), and guided play matches or beats direct instruction on some outcomes (`skene-2022`). Fact, moderate.

**What it does not support**

- Leaving children to work science out alone. Unassisted discovery does worse than explicit teaching (`alfieri-2011`). High.
- Any specific home science curriculum at this age. None was reviewed.

**Resources:** a magnifier, magnets, a ramp and balls, a tub of water, ice, a kitchen scale, a notebook, library nonfiction.

**Activities:** `science-predict-test`, `science-explore-first`, `science-why-chain`, `science-observe-record`.

## Art

**What the evidence supports**

- Very little beyond the art itself. A large review found little rigorous evidence that arts education improves other school results (`winner-2013`). Fact, high.
- Drawing, cutting and modelling practise fine motor control, which correlates with later achievement (`grissmer-2010`). Fact for the correlation, moderate. Any causal role is speculation.

**What it does not support**

- Claims that art makes children smarter or better at school.

**Resources:** real materials in reach: paint, clay, card, tape, glue, good child scissors, decent paper.

**Activities:** `art-open-materials`, `art-observational-drawing`, `art-cut-fold-construct`, `art-look-and-talk`. All four are graded `plausible`.

## Music

**What the evidence supports**

- Music training does not raise cognitive or academic outcomes once studies use random assignment and active controls (`sala-2020`). Fact, high.
- One trial found a small IQ gain after 36 weeks of lessons (`schellenberg-2004`). Fact, low weight, given the meta-analysis.
- Keeping a beat correlates with pre-reading skills in one small study (`woodruff-2014`). Fact, low.
- Four-year-olds were more helpful to each other straight after making music together in one lab study, and a later field study reportedly found no effect (`kirschner-2010`). Fact, low.

**What it does not support**

- Starting lessons early for the sake of intelligence or school results.

**Resources:** a daily singing habit, a keyboard, ukulele or xylophone left where the child can reach it, lessons if the child asks and will practise.

**Activities:** `music-steady-beat`, `music-sing-daily`, `music-make-together`, `music-instrument-exposure`. All four are graded `plausible`.

## Movement and sports

**What the evidence supports**

- Children aged 3-4 should get at least 180 minutes of activity a day with 60 of them moderate-to-vigorous, and from 6 at least 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity (`who-2019`, `us-pag-2018`). Fact, high for the recommendation, moderate for the evidence under it.
- Fundamental movement skills (throw, catch, kick, strike, run, jump, hop, balance) improve with instruction and practice and did not improve with free play alone (`logan-2012`). Fact, moderate.
- Children with better movement skills are more active and fitter later, and the link strengthens with age (`stodden-2008`, `robinson-2015`). Fact, moderate. Mostly correlational.
- Organized sport is beneficial when it stays fun and fits the child's development, and sampling several sports beats early specialization (`aap-sports-2019`, `aap-specialization-2016`). Fact, moderate.
- Swim lessons at ages 1-4 were associated with much lower drowning risk, and paediatric guidance recommends them as one layer of protection (`brenner-2009`, `aap-drowning-2019`). Fact, moderate. The estimate is imprecise.
- Risky outdoor play is associated with more activity and better social health without more fractures (`brussoni-2015`). Fact, moderate. Observational.

**What it does not support**

- Early specialization in one sport (`aap-specialization-2016`). High.
- Expecting team tactics from most children under about 6 (`aap-sports-2019`). Moderate. The age figure is recalled from memory.

**For a child who wants team sports before 6** (inference, moderate): say yes to low-key leagues, coach skills more than strategy, and teach the object-control skills at home, since those are what make games fun and are what free play does not supply.

**Resources:** balls of several sizes, a tee and bat, a low hoop, cones, a bike, swim lessons, a recreation league that changes sport by season.

**Activities:** `movement-throw-catch`, `movement-kick-strike-dribble`, `movement-locomotor-balance`, `movement-active-hour`, `movement-swim`, `movement-team-play`, `movement-risky-play`.

## Social-emotional skills

**What the evidence supports**

- Social competence and self-control in early childhood predict adult outcomes decades later (`jones-2015`, `moffitt-2011`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Emotion knowledge and regulation at 3-4 predict social competence in kindergarten (`denham-2003`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Adults taking over reduces persistence (`leonard-2021`). Fact, moderate.
- Parents' support for autonomy is related to better achievement and wellbeing (`vasquez-2016`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Some activities improve executive function when the challenge keeps rising, with narrow transfer (`diamond-2011`). Fact, moderate. Later results for several programs are mixed.

**What it does not support**

- Treating delay of gratification as a lever (`watts-2018`). High.
- Large effects from growth-mindset praise. Mindset effects on achievement are very small (`sisk-2018`). High.
- That social-emotional behaviour at school entry predicts later academic achievement. It did not in six datasets (`duncan-2007`). It predicts other things (`jones-2015`).

**On pushing a child outside their comfort zone** (inference, low to moderate): no reviewed study tests a home routine of small chosen challenges in typical children. The nearest support is autonomy support and not taking over. `sel-brave-thing` is therefore graded `plausible`. Graded exposure for clinical anxiety was not reviewed.

**Resources:** simple board and card games with turns and a winner, picture books with strong feelings in them, a habit of waiting before helping.

**Activities:** `sel-let-them-struggle`, `sel-process-praise`, `sel-feeling-words`, `sel-rule-games`, `sel-brave-thing`, `sel-plan-do-review`.

## Practical life and independence

**What the evidence supports**

- Chores in kindergarten are associated with later self-competence, prosocial behaviour and math scores (`white-2019`), and chores relate to executive function in a cross-sectional survey (`tepper-2022`). Fact, low. Correlational, and not all studies agree.
- A Montessori preschool program, of which practical-life work is one part, did well in a lottery study (`lillard-2017`). Fact, moderate. The result cannot be attributed to that part.

**What it does not support**

- That chores cause later success. The widely repeated claim rests on correlations. High.

**Why include it anyway** (inference, moderate): the skills are useful in themselves, the cost is low, and doing a real job without an adult taking over is the same mechanism as in `leonard-2021`.

**Resources:** tools the child can reach and manage: a small jug, cloths, a child-size broom, a step stool, low hooks, a picture checklist by the door.

**Activities:** `practical-daily-job`, `practical-self-care-chain`, `practical-pour-carry-clean`, `practical-errand`.

## Cooking

**What the evidence supports**

- Children who helped cook a meal ate more of it, salad especially, in one experiment with 6-10-year-olds (`vanderhorst-2014`). Fact, low.
- Cooking programs for 5-12-year-olds improved food preferences and attitudes in a review of eight weak studies (`hersch-2014`). Fact, low.

**What it does not support**

- Any link from cooking to academic skills. None was reviewed. Counting and measuring while cooking is math practice in a kitchen (inference, moderate).

**Not yet reviewed:** research on repeated tasting of new foods, and age guidance for knives and heat.

**Resources:** a child-safe knife, a peeler, a stable step, measuring cups and spoons, a few picture recipes.

**Activities:** `cooking-prep-vegetables`, `cooking-measure-recipe`, `cooking-mix-crack-spread`, `cooking-taste-new-foods`.

## Nature and outdoors

**What the evidence supports**

- An extra 40 minutes outdoors each school day reduced new cases of short-sightedness over three years in a randomized trial of six-year-olds in China (`he-2015`). Fact, high for that population. Transfer to other populations is an inference, moderate.
- Unstructured nature play is associated with more physical activity and better social and emotional outcomes (`dankiw-2020`). Fact, low.
- A narrative review argues that nature experience improves attention and learning (`kuo-2019`). Fact that the argument is made, low weight.

**What it does not support**

- Strong claims that nature time improves learning. The evidence is weak.

**Resources:** clothes for all weather, a magnifier, a notebook, a bucket, a local place to return to, library nonfiction and field guides.

**Activities:** `nature-outdoor-time`, `nature-notice-walk`, `nature-grow-and-care`, `nature-free-play`, `nature-animal-study`.

## Building and engineering

**What the evidence supports**

- Spatial skills improve with training by nearly half a standard deviation on average, and the gains last and transfer to other spatial tasks (`uttal-2013`). Fact, high.
- Spatial training improves math performance by about a quarter of a standard deviation, more so with physical materials (`hawes-2022`). Fact, moderate. Preschool evidence is thin.
- Copying block constructions relates to early math in three-year-olds (`verdine-2014`). Fact, moderate. Correlational.
- Puzzle play and parents' spatial words predict later spatial skill (`levine-2012`, `pruden-2011`). Fact, low. Correlational.
- Guided play improves spatial vocabulary more than free play (`skene-2022`). Fact, moderate.

**What it does not support**

- That owning building toys is enough. The measured skills are copying a model, rotating shapes and using spatial words, which free building may or may not exercise. Inference, moderate.

**Resources:** unit blocks, interlocking bricks, magnetic tiles, jigsaws of rising piece count, tangrams, pattern blocks, cardboard and tape.

**Activities:** `building-copy-model`, `building-puzzles`, `building-spatial-talk`, `building-design-challenge`, `building-maps`.

## Second language (optional)

**What the evidence supports**

- Children are not confused by two languages, and how well each is learned depends on the amount and quality of exposure (`byers-2013`). Fact, moderate.
- Infants learned a new language's sounds from a live person and not from recordings (`kuhl-2003`). Fact, moderate, for infants.
- Native-like grammar required starting by about 10-12 in a very large online sample (`hartshorne-2018`). Fact, moderate.

**What it does not support**

- A general cognitive advantage from bilingualism (`lehtonen-2018` for adults, `gunnerud-2020` for children). High for adults, moderate for children.
- Proficiency from songs, apps or a weekly class alone. Inference, high, from the exposure findings.

**Decision rule** (inference, moderate): take this on only if the family can supply hours a week of live interaction with a fluent speaker for years. Otherwise the same time is better spent elsewhere.

**Activities:** `language2-live-speaker`, `language2-songs-routines`, `language2-books-with-adult`.

## What is still unknown

- Whether any of these home routines, as written, changes long-run outcomes. None has been tested in the form given here.
- The right dose. Minutes and frequency in the catalog are practical suggestions, not findings.
- Sleep, screen use and nutrition were not reviewed and are outside the thirteen areas.
- Bands other than 48-71 months.
- Sources flagged in `research-sources.md` as recalled from memory or contested by work that was not reviewed.
